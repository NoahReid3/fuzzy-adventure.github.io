The place where a river begins, often a spring, marsh, or meltwater in the upper course, i.e. the river source or headwaters.
The end of a river where it enters the sea, lake, or another river, often with a delta or estuary, i.e. the river mouth.
The wearing away of rock and soil by moving water, ice, wind, or mass movement, i.e. erosion in the Earth’s surface sense.
Where water is forced into cracks in rock and widens them, helping to break rock apart, in rivers and on coasts, hydraulic action.
Where load carried by water scours and grinds the river bed and banks, wearing them down, abrasion in a river.
Where rock fragments carried in the flow hit each other and become rounder and smaller, attrition.
Where slightly acidic water dissolves soluble rock (e.g. limestone) in the channel, solution (or corrosion) in rivers.
The movement of weathered material by the river’s flow, using traction, saltation, suspension, and solution, i.e. river transport processes.
Where large boulders and pebbles are rolled along the river bed, traction.
Where smaller pebbles bounce in short hops along the bed, saltation.
Where fine silt and clay are held up in the water, suspension.
Where the river loses energy and drops its load, building features like point bars and deltas, deposition.
The steep section near the source, with V-shaped valleys, rapids, and vertical erosion dominant, upper course.
The gentler section with meanders and lateral erosion and some deposition, middle course.
The low-lying section with a wide floodplain, meanders, and deposition near the mouth, lower course.
A steep-sided valley with a narrow floor, typical of vertical erosion in the upper course, a V-shaped valley.
Ridges of high land that a meandering river erodes around in interlocking sides, interlocking spurs.
A sudden drop in a river’s long profile, often where hard rock lies over soft, a waterfall.
A deep, narrow valley formed by retreat of a waterfall or rapid vertical erosion in a steep section, a gorge.
A bend in a river’s course with erosion on the outside and deposition on the inside, a meander.
A cut-off bend that becomes a curved lake beside the new channel, an oxbow lake.
The flat area of land beside a river that floods when the river overflows, built of deposited alluvium, floodplain.
A raised bank along a river built from repeated flooding and deposition of silt, a levee.
A fan or fingers of deposited material at a river mouth where the flow slows and spreads, a delta.
The tidal mouth of a river where fresh and salt water mix, often funnel-shaped, an estuary.
The volume of water passing a point per second (m³/s), river discharge.
The speed of water flow in a channel, usually m/s, velocity.
A graph of river discharge over time after rainfall, showing rising limb, peak, and falling limb, a flood hydrograph.
The time between peak rainfall and peak river discharge, lag time.
The maximum discharge shown on a hydrograph during a flood event, peak discharge.
The movement of water from the surface into the soil and rock, infiltration.
Water flowing over the land surface when rainfall exceeds infiltration rate, surface runoff (overland flow).
Rain caught and held by vegetation before it reaches the ground, interception.
The slow movement of water through soil and rock toward the river, groundwater flow (throughflow and baseflow).
How easily water can pass through rock or soil, permeability.
The narrow zone where land meets the sea, including cliffs, beaches, and estuaries, the coast.
The removal of material from the coast by waves and other processes, coastal erosion.
Where waves compress air in cracks and widen them, breaking rock, hydraulic action at the coast.
Where wave-borne pebbles scrape and smooth the cliff and bedrock, abrasion at the coast.
Where rock pieces in the surf zone hit each other and become smaller and rounder, attrition at the coast.
Movement of beach material along the shore in the direction of the prevailing longshore current, longshore drift.
Where waves lose energy and drop sand, shingle, or mud, building beaches and spits, coastal deposition.
An area of resistant rock sticking out into the sea, often with caves and arches, a headland.
An inlet of sea between two headlands where softer rock has been eroded, a bay.
A steep rock face at the coast formed by marine erosion, a cliff.
A wide, flat rock platform left in front of a retreating cliff as the cliff is cut back, a wave-cut platform.
A hole in a cliff base widened by wave action, a sea cave.
Where two caves on either side of a headland join to form a rock arch, an arch.
An isolated pillar of rock left when an arch roof collapses, a stack.
The base of a stack after the top has fallen, a stump.
A deposit of sand or shingle between high and low tide, a beach.
A narrow bank of sand or shingle joined to the land at one end, built by longshore drift, a spit.
A ridge of deposited material across a bay or river mouth, closing off water behind, a bar (e.g. offshore or bay bar).
A spit that links an island to the mainland, a tombolo.
Shallow water trapped behind a bar or spit, often brackish, a lagoon.
A low, long wave with a strong swash that deposits material on the beach, a constructive (swash-dominated) wave.
A high, steep wave with a strong backwash that erodes the beach, a destructive (backwash-dominated) wave.
The movement of water and sediment up the beach after a wave breaks, swash.
The movement of water and sediment back down the beach, backwash.
The distance of open water over which wind drives waves; longer fetch means larger waves, fetch.
A hard wall built on the beach or cliff foot to stop waves reaching land, a sea wall.
A fence-like structure at right angles to the beach to trap moving sediment and slow longshore loss, a groyne.
Large boulders placed at the cliff foot or on the beach to absorb wave energy, rock armour, rip-rap.
Wire baskets filled with stone used to stabilise slopes or the cliff base, a gabion.
Letting the coast move landward and flooding low land instead of hard defence, managed retreat, coastal realignment.
Flooding of coastal land from high sea level, big waves, or a storm surge, coastal flooding.
A dome of high sea under a low-pressure storm, pushed onshore, raising water level and often causing coastal flood, a storm surge.
Waves slow and bend in shallower water so they line up with the coast; energy focuses on headlands, wave refraction.
Downslope movement of material under gravity, e.g. slumps and slides, mass movement, mass wastage.
Where a mass of material rotates and moves along a curved slip surface, a slump.
Where rock or debris falls quickly down a steep face, a rockfall.
Where wind builds a ridge of sand inland from a beach, often with marram grass, a dune, sand dune.
A low-energy coastal salt-tolerant community (e.g. cord grass) in the tidal zone, a salt marsh.
The living and non-living systems of the coast interacting, e.g. dunes, rock pools, and marshes, a coastal ecosystem.
Defences and land-use planning that protect people and the environment in the long term, sustainable coastal management.
The state of the atmosphere at a place over a short time (hours to days), i.e. day-to-day conditions, weather.
The average pattern of weather over a long period (30+ years) in a place, i.e. climate.
A measure of how hot or cold the air (or a surface) is, usually in °C, temperature.
Any moisture falling from the air to the ground: rain, snow, sleet, or hail, precipitation.
The force of the air pushing on a surface, shown on weather maps, often in millibars, air (barometric) pressure.
The amount of water vapour in the air, often shown as relative humidity, humidity.
The change of liquid water to water vapour, adding moisture to the air, evaporation.
The change of water vapour to liquid or ice, e.g. forming clouds, condensation.
The continuous movement of water between the sea, air, and land, driven by the sun, the hydrological (water) cycle, water cycle.
Rain caused when warm moist air is forced to rise over mountains, cooling to condense, relief (orographic) rainfall.
