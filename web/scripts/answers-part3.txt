Rain when warm and cold air masses meet at a front; the warm air rises, cools, and condenses, frontal (cyclonic) rainfall.
Lines on a weather map joining places of equal air pressure, used to show pressure pattern, isobars.
A low-pressure system (cyclone) with rising air, cloud, and often rain and strong winds, in mid-latitudes called a depression.
A high-pressure system with sinking air, often clear skies and light winds, an anticyclone.
The natural process where certain gases in the atmosphere trap outgoing heat, warming the surface, the greenhouse effect.
The long-term rise in average global temperatures linked to human extra greenhouse gases, global warming.
Long-term change in temperature, rainfall, storm patterns, or sea level, including human and natural causes, climate change.
The total greenhouse gas emissions caused by a person, product, or place, often in CO₂ equivalent, a carbon footprint.
An energy source that is naturally renewed (e.g. sun, wind, water) and not used up on a human timescale, renewable energy.
A long period when rainfall is much below normal, causing water shortage and crop stress, a drought.
When water covers land that is usually dry, from heavy rain, storm surge, or snowmelt, a flood.
Unusually severe heat, cold, storm, or rainfall away from the long-term average, an extreme weather event.
A natural process (e.g. earthquake, flood) that can harm people and property, a natural hazard.
The chance that a hazard will occur and cause harm, combining hazard, exposure, and vulnerability, risk in geography.
Actions to reduce causes or size of a hazard or emissions, e.g. building codes or cutting CO₂, mitigation.
Changes in behaviour, design, or planning to cope with hazard or climate effects that cannot be fully stopped, adaptation.
A sudden shaking of the ground caused by movement at a plate boundary or along a fault, releasing stress, an earthquake.
Movement and stress release at plate boundaries or along faults when rock breaks and slips, what causes earthquakes.
A line where two tectonic plates meet, with different relative motion, a plate boundary.
Where plates move apart; magma may rise and new crust forms, e.g. mid-ocean ridge, constructive (divergent) boundary.
Where plates collide; one may be subducted, with earthquakes, volcanoes, and mountains, destructive (convergent) boundary.
Where plates slide past each other horizontally, with earthquakes but little volcanism, conservative (transform) boundary.
The point on the Earth’s surface directly above the earthquake focus, where shaking is often strongest, the epicentre.
The point inside the Earth where the earthquake slip starts, at depth, the focus (or hypocentre).
A vent through which magma reaches the surface, often building a cone, a volcano.
Molten rock stored beneath the surface, within the Earth, magma.
Molten rock that has erupted onto the surface, lava.
An intense rotating low-pressure storm over tropical oceans, with very strong winds and heavy rain, tropical cyclone or hurricane.
Warm sea (about 26 °C+), moisture, and Coriolis spin allow a low to deepen into a rotating storm, causes of tropical storms.
A series of very long-wavelength sea waves, often from undersea earthquakes or landslides, a tsunami.
Displacement of water from seafloor earthquakes, volcanoes, or large landslides into the sea, what creates a tsunami.
A logarithmic scale of earthquake size based on shaking recorded (used historically), the Richter scale (magnitude scale).
The amount of energy released at the source of an earthquake, shown as a single magnitude number, what magnitude measures.
An instrument that records ground motion and draws a seismogram, a seismograph.
A broad, gently sloping volcano made of runny lava flows, with non-explosive eruptions, a shield volcano.
A steep-sided volcano made of layers of lava and ash, with explosive and viscous lava, a composite (strato) volcano.
A bowl-shaped depression at a volcano’s summit, a crater.
The release of magma, gas, and ash from a volcano, an eruption.
Ash and fine particles blown high into the atmosphere during a volcanic eruption, an ash cloud.

Problems that follow the primary event, e.g. disease after flooding or fires after earthquake, secondary effect.

Rebuilding, insurance, better building standards, and recovery over months to years, long-term response.

Using science to state when/where a hazard may happen, hazard prediction.
Education, drills, and plans so people know what to do before a hazard, preparedness.
Continuous observation of volcanoes, earthquakes, or weather to give early warning, hazard monitoring.
Moving people out of a dangerous area before or during a hazard, evacuation.
An area mapped as likely to be hit by a hazard, with rules on building and use, a hazard zone.
The conditions of a place or group that make harm from a hazard more likely, vulnerability.
The ability of a community or system to recover from or adapt to a hazard, resilience.
Money, goods, or experts given to help after a hazard, from governments or NGOs, aid in hazard management.
Help sent from other countries after a disaster, e.g. relief flights and funds, international aid.
Actions by local people, communities, and councils right after a hazard, local response.
National laws, funding, and military or civil defence organised by the state, government response.
Rebuilding infrastructure, homes, and livelihoods after damage, reconstruction.
Identifying probability and impact of hazards to guide planning and insurance, risk assessment.
A rise in sea surface under a storm that floods coasts when combined with wind and waves, a storm surge (hazard context).
The calm, clear, low-wind centre of a tropical storm, the eye.
A scale (e.g. Saffir–Simpson) that groups storms by wind speed and damage potential, category scale for storms.
When water covers land as a harmful event, including from rivers or sea, flooding as a hazard.

Storm surge, high tides, or low pressure raising sea level onto the land, causes of coastal flooding.
Material moving downslope as a coherent or broken mass on a slip surface, a landslide.

Rocks or debris falling freely down a steep slope, a rockfall (slope hazard context).
Design and materials that reduce damage in earthquakes, wind, or floods, hazard-resistant building.
Official messages and technology that warn people before a hazard arrives, early warning system.
Immediate food, shelter, and supplies after a disaster, disaster relief.
Temporary housing for people who have lost homes, emergency shelter.
Food supplied to people who cannot obtain it after a disaster, food aid.
Doctors, medicine, and hospitals sent to treat the injured, medical aid.
Teams that find and help people trapped after a collapse or flood, search and rescue.
Broken roads, power, water, and communications after a hazard, infrastructure damage.
Lost jobs, business, and reconstruction cost from a hazard, economic loss.
Harm to ecosystems, soil, water, and air from a hazard or its effects, environmental damage.
People forced to leave home but still in the country, or mass movement away from danger, population displacement.
A camp of temporary shelter for refugees or displaced people, often with UN or NGO support, refugee camp.
A map showing where different hazards are likely or severe, for planning, a hazard map.
Using planning rules to ban or limit building in dangerous areas, zoning in hazard management.
Spreading the cost of damage through policies that pay out after events, insurance in hazard management.
Local training, plans, and networks so people know what to do, community preparedness.
A practice exercise to test response to earthquakes, fires, or storms, a drill in hazard preparation.
Structural and non-structural actions that lower probability or impact of harm, risk reduction.
