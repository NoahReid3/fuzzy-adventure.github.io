The average number of people per unit of area, usually people per km², showing how crowded a place is.
Total population ÷ total land area of the same unit (often people per km²).
A situation where a population is larger than the local resources, environment, or services can sustainably support (or exceeds a sustainable level).
A situation with too few people to use available resources, jobs, and services fully; often means labour and tax shortages.
The number of live births per 1,000 people in the population in one year.
The number of deaths per 1,000 people in the population in one year.
The change in population size caused by the balance between births and deaths (it excludes migration in its narrow sense).
It is found from birth rate minus death rate, or (births − deaths) in a time period, often expressed as a % or per 1,000.
The count of all people in an area (or country) at a given time; a single total at one moment.
The number of deaths of children under one year old per 1,000 live births in a year.
The average number of years a person in a place or group is expected to live, if current death rates continue.
The ratio of people not in the working age group (dependents) to the working-age population, often as a % or a ratio to 100 workers.
A population structure with a high and rising share of older people, usually linked to low birth rates and long life expectancy.
A population with a high proportion of young people and a high birth rate, often shown as a wide base on a population pyramid.
The movement of people to live in a new place, usually when crossing a clear boundary, for a long period or permanently.
The movement of people into a place or country to live (in-migration).
The movement of people out of a place or country to live elsewhere (out-migration).
Negative conditions that make people want to leave a place, e.g. poverty, war, or lack of jobs.
Advantages that attract people to a place, e.g. jobs, family links, or better services; reasons to come to a destination.
Movement where people are compelled to move by conflict, human rights abuse, or disaster, without a real free choice; includes refugees in many cases.
Migration people choose, mainly for economic or family reasons, when they are not forced to go.
Movement of people from rural areas to towns and cities, often for jobs, services, and education; rural-to-urban shift.
The growth in the share of a country’s (or world’s) population living in urban areas, and the building of more towns and cities.
The way a population is spread out over a region, e.g. clustered, even, or sparse; the pattern in space, often mapped as density.
A diagram (often a pair of back-to-back or stacked bars) of population by five-year age groups and sex, showing a population’s structure.
A very young age structure, high birth rate, and rapid population growth, shown by many people in the youngest groups at the base.
A low share of people in the oldest age groups, e.g. because of a falling population, low life expectancy, or past high death rates; often a small top to the diagram.
The maximum population (or use level) that an area’s environment and resources can support over a long time without long-term damage.
Rules and programmes by a government to influence population (birth rate, age structure) or sometimes migration, e.g. through laws, cash benefits, or education.
A policy to encourage a higher birth rate, e.g. through child benefits, parental leave, or pro-family messages.
A policy to limit births, e.g. one-child type rules, free contraception, and education to reduce family size.
The long-run fertility (about 2.1 children per couple in HICs) that would keep population size stable if nothing else changes.
A migrant who moves mainly to get better-paid work, rather than to flee war or persecution.
A person who has been forced to leave their country, due to a well-founded fear of persecution, war, or violence, and seeks another state’s protection.
A person who has left their own country, applied in another for protection, and is waiting for a decision on whether they are a refugee.
The annual % change in total population, including natural change and, in full definitions, net migration, shown as a % of the total.
An official count of everyone in a country, usually on a set day, to collect data on who lives where; often every five or ten years.
A model of how total population, birth rate, and death rate change in stages as a country’s economy and society develop, from high BR/DR to low BR/DR and ageing.
People moving to live in another part of the same country, without changing country, e.g. to find a job; internal migration.
People moving to live in another country, across an international border, for a long time or to settle in that country.
A place where people have their homes, from a few houses to a large city, i.e. a group of buildings where people live.
The physical features of a place where a settlement is built, e.g. good drainage, water supply, and defendable or dry ground.
How a place is set relative to other places, e.g. on a route, near resources, and its links to larger centres (access and connectivity), i.e. its situation in the area.
A small settlement, usually in the countryside, with few services and a low population density, i.e. rural.
A large, built-up place, non-agricultural in main function, with many people, jobs, and services, i.e. a town or city in character; urban.
A very small cluster of a few homes, the smallest in the size hierarchy, with few or no services.
A settlement larger than a hamlet, with a small set of local services, e.g. a shop, school, and church, often in rural areas.
A medium-sized place with a wider than local set of services and jobs, but smaller than a city, i.e. a town, not a village or a city in scale.
A large place with a wide range of services, jobs, and often regional government or culture; often above a set population or by function, i.e. a city.
A very large city, usually over 10 million people, often formed by merging of built-up areas, i.e. a huge city region, megacity.
The ordering of settlements from largest (e.g. city) with the most and highest-level services, down to the smallest (e.g. hamlet) with only basic or few.
The area from which a settlement or a service draws its customers, workers, or support; the area “influenced” by that centre, often in travel time.
In geography, a part of the economy (or a business) that does not make physical products but provides something people use, e.g. retail, health, or transport.
The minimum number of people needed in an area to support a service and keep that service viable, i.e. the minimum “market” size.
The maximum distance people will travel to use a service, or the distance a good or service is supplied, often in km or minutes, i.e. service range.
The type of use an area of land is put to, e.g. housing, industry, farming, or transport, as shown on a land-use map, i.e. what the land is used for.
The central zone of a city, with offices, key shops, and very high land values; the core business and retail heart of the urban area, Central Business District.
The zone of older, often inner-city, housing and mixed use around the CBD, sometimes with decline or regeneration, i.e. the inner city ring.
The edge of the built-up area where the city meets the countryside, often with new housing and industry, i.e. the urban-rural edge.
The movement of middle-class and wealthier people from inner cities to suburbs and the urban fringe, often for a better house and environment.
The spreading of a city outward over rural land, often in a low-density, car-based form, with loss of open land, i.e. urban growth without tight planning, sprawl.
People and investment moving back into the inner city, often to new flats, converted warehouses, and waterfronts, i.e. city-centre living reviving, re-urbanisation.
The movement of people, jobs, and services out of big cities to smaller towns, villages, or the countryside, often for quality of life, counter-urbanisation.
A poor, self-built, often unplanned area of makeshift homes, with little or poor services, common on the edge of many LIC/MIC cities, i.e. shanty town, informal area.
A settlement of homes built on land the residents do not legally own, without planning permission, often with few services, i.e. squatter or informal housing.
Economic activities that are not officially registered, taxed, or regulated, e.g. street selling and unregistered work, the informal (shadow) sector.
Economic activities that are legal, registered, and taxed, with normal labour and business rules, i.e. the official economy, formal sector.
The planned improvement and rebuilding of an old urban area, e.g. new housing, shops, and public space on run-down land, i.e. urban renewal or regeneration.
The process by which richer people move into a low-income area, property prices rise, and original residents are priced out, changing the area’s social character.
Land that has been built on or used for development before, e.g. an old factory site, re-used before building on new countryside, i.e. previously developed, brownfield.
Land that has not been built on before, usually in the open countryside, used for new development, i.e. a greenfield site, open field site.
A person who lives in one place (e.g. a suburb) and travels to work or study in another, usually daily, i.e. a daily journey-to-work person.
The highest land and property values, usually in or near the CBD, where people pay most to be near the most accessible sites, i.e. the peak value point.
A zone planned or used mainly for factories, industry, and warehouses, with limited housing, an industrial or factory zone.
A zone where most land is for housing, with little heavy industry, i.e. a mainly residential or housing area.
A zone of shops, offices, and services, often in or near the city centre, i.e. retail and business, commercial use.
A linked system of roads, railways, paths, and ports (and sometimes air) that moves people, goods, and information between places, i.e. the transport system.
The control and design of how land in towns and cities is used, through plans, transport, and zoning, to make places work better, i.e. town or urban planning.
A city that tries to meet people’s present social, economic, and environmental needs without stopping future people meeting theirs, i.e. sustainable development at the city level.
When vehicles on a road or network are so many that flow slows, delays grow, and time and fuel are wasted, i.e. road congestion, traffic jams, traffic congestion.
A body of water flowing in a natural channel, from a source to a sea, lake, or other water body, i.e. a natural watercourse, river, or large stream.
The area of land drained by a river and all of its tributaries, bounded by a watershed; the river’s catchment or drainage basin.
A line of high land separating two drainage basins, i.e. a drainage divide, watershed, or catchment edge.
A smaller stream or river that flows into a larger one, a feeder or joining stream, which is called a tributary.
The point where two rivers or streams meet and their channel waters combine, a river junction, i.e. a confluence.
